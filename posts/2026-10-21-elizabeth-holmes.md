title: Elizabeth Holmes
category: Essay
excerpt: An uncomfortable mirror
---

I watched the Elizabeth Holmes documentary yesterday, You can see everything, by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim. I watched it at the *theater*, meaning I drove into town specifically to watch it. I had to know... what kind of documentary film has spoilers? Spoilers you have to conscientiously avoid online? After sitting in my seat, riveted for over three hours - without taking a bathroom break - I get it. But that's not what inspired me to write this morning. See, I know what Elizabeth Holmes' story means to me, I know what it reflects in my understanding of the world and - to be real - in myself. And it should come as no surprise that we all see different things when we look into that mirror, smudged with the limitations of our experience. I want to describe it for you, my imperfect view into that mirror, and, through what it transmits or omits, know more about who I am and who we are.

I have no idea where to start. Starting at the beginning feels like a trap. Starting at the end is impossible, because I'm not sure where I'm going yet. I need to choose somewhere in the middle. Grad school? My first internship out of high school? That messed up Lasers in Surgery and Medicine conference in Florida? My first year at Apple? Fuck it, it doesn't matter. The point is the people advancing science and technology must master the masquerade of egos, buzz words, and hype. I once sought advice from two older white guys at an Incubator in St. Louis, Missouri, and was told in barely coded language that I didn't have *it*. *It* had nothing to do with the idea I had spent years developing, prototyping, and studying. In the spirit of this essay, I won't go into the actual idea. You can read about it [here](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51219768_Optical_polarimetry_for_noninvasive_glucose_sensing_enabled_by_Sagnac_interferometry#fullTextFileContent) if you want. By contrast, Elizabeth Holmes has *it*. And if I could speak directly to the start-up incubators and investors for a minute, Elizabeth Holmes is exactly the person you've been grooming. She fully embodied what you asked for and nothing else. What do you see when you look into the mirror of her story? By emptying herself of anything else, she reflects your culture, your world, without distortion. In surrendering to her narcissists - her Sunnys, her Billys - she perfectly reflects our culture's patriarchal views and finds a hollow refuge. Doubling down on tech start-up culture, feeding its needs like it's another narcissistic lover, she traumatizes herself again and again, sticking David Blaine's ice pick into her hand over and over. Nathan Fielder was brilliant to use that analogy in the film. (Definitely worth the Google if you aren't familiar with this scene.)

It's fair to ask at this point - do I have a chip on my shoulder? You can make up your own mind on that. Succeeding in the tech start-up world would not have brought me happiness, and I have only grown more supportive of myself for leaving it as the years go by. My 27-ish year-old self didn't have the words for the sadness and disappointment at the time - not just from the Incubator experience but from the decades of brushing up against the masquerade that I didn't have a mask for. Whether it be a byproduct of capitalism or tech culture ego and hype, this black hole for the ephemeral is never satiated, and I gladly pursue my happiness and meaning elsewhere. Do I still bristle at the delusion, faux meritocracy, faux justice? I suppose I do. Why else would I write this, I wonder.

So is Elizabeth Holmes my femme fatale? Absolutely fucking not. I don't have patience for anyone who plays into narcissism and patriarchy, regardless of whether she plays the aggressor or the victim. The fact that anyone plays at all is a deal breaker for me. Which leads me to the next reflection I see in Elizabeth Holmes - my own. For all my innate inadequacies in the game, I tried to play. I traumatized myself over and over again, as I tried to navigate the use of my emotions for my own gain, the use of my vulnerability to garner support. As an autistic person, the concept of "my own gain" may be different than most... I become laser-focused on a puzzle, a problem to solve. My emotions are triggered by inaccurate information, willful misdirection, and the dissonance of intermingling personal status, reputation, and blame with troubleshooting and discovery. I love being right, but I do not feel any victory in winning an argument when I'm wrong, and my standard for right and wrong can be brutal. I drove my supervisors crazy by metaphorically shooting myself in the foot over and over again if I sensed a hint that I wasn't perfectly correct. I couldn't see that my standard was different than anyone else's and I was duped over and over again by others' self-confidence. All the same, I tried, and I mimicked to the best of my ability the tricks of the trade. After my first year at Apple, though, I dissociated. To this day, I can't remember who I was that year. Over the next few years at Apple, I learned that I didn't have to be someone else to be successful. I didn't have to agonize over losing an argument of words, a contest of egos. I didn't have to exert faux authority to be respected by my peers. I feel immense gratitude for the people who taught me this just by embodying it themselves. What does this have to do with Elizabeth Holmes? Maybe nothing. But when I see how differently she purports herself now, post Theranos, post trial, post motherhood, I see the dissociation I experienced myself. I wonder if anyone on the outside would believe this version of me if they had met me before. What I see in Elizabeth now, is someone who has dissociated not only from the past version of herself but from the belief in her own autonomy. 

Autonomy - such a tricky concept for those of us socialized as women. If you haven't experienced it, I can understand why you might not believe it. The collective gaslighting is beautifully alluring. When you asked to cut the grass when you were six, did your dad say no to protect you from the possibility you would cut your toes off? Did he then encourage your brother to help him outside when he was five? Did he take your brothers out camping, teach them to tie knots, build a fire, set up a tent, catch fish, while asking you to stay home so your mom could watch Little Women with her daughter? When you entered engineering school, and your male peers had already spent years fixing up engines, stuff your dad invited your brothers to do but not you, did you question if you were good enough? When did you start opting out or curling up in the comfort of being taken care of? And when did you learn that your looks and your body were the means to be safe in the world, like a token to trade in for being kept in a cocoon of dependence and blamelessness? When your dad told him forcefully that he better take good care of you, did you feel the last of your sense of autonomy evaporate in a wave of warmth and love? If it were expected of you, would you accept the freedom to pursue your special interests, at the cost of agency over your body and your life decisions? Respect and compassion to those of us who say no. It's not an easy road to retain your sense of self in the onslaught of loving patriarchy. Reject the carrot and the stick falls on you like an anvil - the weight of which crushed me when I got a divorce. 

As I watch Elizabeth in her relationships, with her partner Billy and even with her interviewer Nathan, I see her pour herself into that patriarchal cocoon, with the trust and naivety of someone who desperately seeks to free herself of the illusion of autonomy. Will we accept this version of her? If she lobotomizes self-empowerment and agency from her image, real or imagined, will we see her humanity? Watching this dollhouse mother, deferential in her support of the worst of our inclinations, I find I don't care if the act is real or performative. Because either way, she is showing us a very real manifestation of ourselves.

I reflect a lot on my complicity. As we put Elizabeth Holmes in prison and tell ourselves that she is different than we are, do we allow ourselves to see our complicity? Or do we excise not the worst of ourselves but the mirror that reflects it? When we see everything, do we revel and gape, only to look away? 

This is my experience of this person, from a comfortable distance, only indirectly affected by her actions and the worlds that orbit her. The tech world, the artful masquerade, the reflections and echoes of my own actions and experience. Her story pulls me into myself, a deep emotional well, that ultimately has nothing to do with her and everything to do with my deliberate self-absorption. Her story is another invitation to dive into that well with fuller presence and awareness. Nathan concludes his documentary with footage of him swimming with Elizabeth in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of San Diego. They appear as lovers, playfully clinging to each other as the waves envelop them again and again. A dance with our inner child, embracing ignorance like a blanket, that pure love and bliss trapped within ourselves, yet still an ocean between our dreams and awakening.
